Experiment 1 · November 2026

More thinking costs more tokens, not always more accuracy.

I ran four open AI models on my own graphics card and counted the tokens each task used: thinking off and on, a single answer and an agent. The price per token stayed fixed. Only the tokens per task and the success rate changed.

Correction, 12 November 2026. Qwen3.8-27B writes 67 tokens per second on this card, not 20, and the gap between the fastest and the slowest model is about 4 times, not 14. The cause was a vLLM flag of mine, not the model. Token counts and accuracy are unaffected. Finding 5 has the details and keeps the published numbers next to the corrected ones.

  • 4 open models
  • 12 settings
  • 4 tasks
  • 2,540 answers scored against a key
  • 1 RTX 5090
  • Run on 8 November 2026

The short version

Tokens per task decide the bill, and they move a lot.

  • ×6.8tokens with thinking onSmall model, hard maths, and 20 points less accuracy.
  • ×11tokens as an agentBut only ×3.5 the cost, because input is cheap.
  • ×128cost, frontier vs small modelSame school maths questions, at list prices (projection).
  • ×4speed gap on one GPU287 against 67 tokens per second. Corrected 12 November 2026; the video says ×14.

Measure tokens per task and the success rate before you pay for more thinking, a bigger model or an agent.

Finding 1 · Thinking

More thinking means more tokens, not always more accuracy.

The same fifty hard maths problems, with thinking off and on. Tokens per problem rose 1.3 to 6.8 times. Accuracy moved by −20 to +14 points.

The small model thought until it ran out of room on 10 of the 50 problems, and solved fewer than without thinking. On a hundred school maths problems, thinking bought one to three points for 1.3 to 6.5 times the tokens.

Turn thinking on only where it raises the success rate on your own test.

Finding 2 · Classifying

The model that used the most tokens was not the most accurate.

A hundred customer messages, each to be sorted into one of 77 intents. The best result came from gpt-oss-20b at low effort: 87 % for 570 tokens per message. Qwen3.8-27B at its default effort scored 79 % for 676 tokens. The small model with thinking on reached 75 % for 2,856.

Test each task on several models before you pick one.

Finding 3 · Agents

An agent sends its whole context again at every step.

As an agent with three tools, Qwen3.8-27B answered all twenty invoice questions correctly, with 3.8 calls and 5,613 tokens per question. A single answer to a school maths problem took 494 tokens: the agent used 11 times as many, on a different task.

Each call sends everything seen so far. 93 % of the tokens are input, which costs a fifth of output at list prices. So the cost rises about 3.5 times, not 11.

Use an agent only when the task needs to look something up.

Finding 4 · Cost

Which model answers moves the bill more than thinking or agents.

Priced at OpenAI’s list prices, a million school maths questions cost €144 with the small model and €18,325 with the frontier model: 128 times more. Thinking at xhigh adds a quarter (€22,859). The agent, on its invoice questions, comes to €64,643.

The token counts come from the open models and the prices from a commercial price list, so this is a projection, not a bill.

Route each task to the smallest model that passes your test.

Finding 5 · Speed

On your own hardware, time is the bill.

One request at a time on one RTX 5090: gpt-oss-20b wrote 287 tokens per second, Qwen3.5-4B 157, Gemma 4 31B 71 and Qwen3.8-27B 67. That is about 4 times apart.

Correction, 12 November 2026. The video (at 5:25) and the first version of this page say 20 tokens per second for Qwen3.8-27B and a gap of 14 times. That was my mistake, not the model’s: I had started vLLM with the flag --enforce-eager, which turns off CUDA graphs, so the GPU waited for the CPU on every token and sat idle more than half the time. Measured again with the flag fixed, same build and same checkpoint: 67 tokens per second. The video cannot be changed; its description and pinned comment carry the correction. Token counts and accuracy are not affected, because the flag changes when the kernels run, not what they compute: a 100-message spot check gave 73% correct against the published 71%, at the same token count. The published rows are kept next to the new ones in the correction note on GitHub.

Thinking multiplies the wait. The small model took 19 seconds per hard problem with thinking off, and 162 seconds with it on.

On your own hardware, measure the time per task, not only the tokens.

  • Hardware. One NVIDIA RTX 5090 with 32 GB, my own, at home. No employer hardware and no employer data.
  • Software. vLLM 0.26.0, one model at a time; Qwen3.8-27B ran on a vLLM development build for its 4-bit kernels and, by my mistake, with CUDA graphs off (--enforce-eager), which cost it two thirds of its speed and nothing else; its speed was measured again on 12 November 2026. Context up to 32,768 tokens, answers up to 28,000.
  • Sampling. The model makers’ recommended settings for each mode. Items sampled with seed 42, requests with seed 42, one run per item.
  • Counting. Tokens per item are prompt plus completion tokens, summed over every call. Reasoning tokens are part of the completion. An answer that hit the limit counts as wrong.

Limits

A measurement of this setup, not of AI in general.

  • Open models only. Commercial models count and reason differently. The multipliers are the point, not the absolute token counts.
  • Benchmarks stand in for work. The tasks have answer keys, real business tasks rarely do. The agent task is synthetic.
  • Small samples. One run per item, 20 agent questions and 50 hard problems. A second seed would show the spread.
  • Effort is an instruction. For Qwen3.8-27B, “effort” is a line in the system prompt, not a token budget, and “medium” adds nothing.
  • Euros are a projection. Measured tokens at commercial list prices, which change.
