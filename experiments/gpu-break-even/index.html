Experiment 2 · November 2026

Count your tasks before you buy a GPU.

Should a team buy a graphics card for its AI models, or pay a provider per token? I loaded my own card until users would start to wait, scored every answer, and priced the same work at a German cloud. The answer is a number of tasks a day.

  • 2 open models
  • 4 tasks
  • 84 runs
  • 131,939 answers scored against a key
  • 1 RTX 5090
  • Run on 13 November 2026
Code and data on GitHub

The short version

Below a number of tasks a day, the API is cheaper. Above it, owning is.

  • 2,850 · agent questions a working day

    Below 2,850 agent questions a day, paying per token is cheaper than owning a machine.

    An agent question is one that the AI answers by looking things up with tools, here in a set of invoices. A German cloud sells the same open model for €0.45 per million tokens of input and €0.65 per million of output. One agent question used 4,678 tokens of input and 382 of output on my card, so the cloud would charge €0.00235 for it: about a quarter of a cent.

    My machine cost €4,423 without VAT. Spread over 3 years that is €1,474 a year. Standing ready all year with the model loaded, it draws 85 watts, which costs €176 a year at €0.2366 per kWh. Together €1,650 a year, or €6.60 on each of 250 working days.

    Divide the machine’s €6.60 a day by the cloud’s quarter of a cent per question, and you get about 2,850. Below that the cloud bill is smaller than the machine; above it, the machine wins. The extra electricity of answering is tiny (€0.00003 a question) and already counted.

    Example: a team of 50 people asking 20 agent questions each sends 1,000 a day. The cloud charges about €2.35 for them; the machine costs €6.60 whether it is used or not.

    Count your agent questions per working day. Below this line, keep paying per token.

  • 7,500 · agent questions a day, with staff

    Count the people, and owning pays only from about 7,500 agent questions a day.

    A machine of your own needs someone to install updates, restart it when it hangs and check that it still works. My machine needs between 1 and 5 hours a month, and agents already do most of that work.

    At €45 an hour, the German average labour cost, 5 hours cost €225 a month, or €2,700 a year. Over 250 working days that is €10.80 a day, more than the €6.60 for the machine and its power.

    So owning now costs €17.40 a working day. Divided by the cloud’s €0.00235 per question, the break-even becomes about 7,500, 2.6 times the figure without staff. With only 1 hour a month it is about 3,800.

    The cloud includes this work in its price. That is why staff time moves the line more than electricity or the price of the card.

    Ask who will look after the machine and for how many hours. Put that into the calculation before the electricity price.

  • 4,340 · agent questions an hour on one card

    Working on 16 questions at once, one card answers about 4,340 an hour.

    A graphics card can work on many requests in the same pass. With one question at a time it answered 540 agent questions an hour. With 16 at once it answered 4,340, 8 times as many.

    The limit is the users’ patience. I counted a request as fast enough when the first word arrived within 2 seconds and the answer then flowed at 10 words a second or more, and I allowed only 1 call in 100 to be slower. At 16 questions at once, 100 % of the calls were fast enough. At 21 it was 92.5 %, and the card did not answer any more questions: they only waited longer.

    In an 8-hour day that is about 34,700 agent questions, 12 times the break-even of 2,850. It assumes the questions arrive evenly; real traffic comes in bursts.

    One card is rarely too small for a team. The question is whether you have enough work to keep it busy.

  • 99% · correct under full load

    A busy card answers as well as an idle one.

    Every answer was checked against a fixed answer key, with no AI judging another AI. Under full load, with 16 agent questions at once, the card answered 99.4 % of 673 questions correctly. With one question at a time it was 98.8 % of 84.

    The other three tasks behaved the same way: under load, accuracy stayed within 3.3 points of one request at a time. The largest drop, on long solutions, comes from a tiny sample at one request at a time (18 problems), and its uncertainty range includes zero.

    This is expected: sharing the card between requests changes when the work is done, not what is computed. One limit: my card runs a compressed, 4-bit copy of the model, which I did not compare with the full-precision version a cloud may serve.

Finding 1 · The break-even

Owning pays from about 2,850 agent questions a working day.

An API bills every question. A machine of your own costs the same every day, whether it works or waits. So the two lines cross at a number of tasks a day, and below that number the API is cheaper.

For an agent question, one that looks things up with tools, the API charges about a quarter of a cent (€0.24 per 100). My test machine costs €6.60 per working day for the hardware and its power. The lines cross at 2,850 questions a day.

Then count the people. My machine needs between 1 and 5 hours of care a month. At €45 an hour, 5 hours add €10.80 a working day, more than the hardware itself, and the crossing moves to 7,500.

Count your tasks per working day before you compare prices per token.

Finding 2 · By task

Agents reach the line first. A team’s chat never does.

The break-even depends on what the API charges for one task. An agent question is expensive at the API, because the agent sends its whole context again at every step: 89 % of its price is input. So owning pays from 2,850 a day.

A short answer costs the API about an eighth of that. Owning needs 22,400 short answers a day to pay, and 32,400 sorted messages. Long worked solutions sit in between at 5,350.

As an illustration: a team of 50 people asking 20 short questions each sends 1,000 a day, a twentieth of what owning needs. 250 people with 20 agent questions each send 5,000: above the line with hardware only, below it once staff is counted.

Compare each kind of task with its own break-even. One number for “AI usage” hides the answer.

Finding 3 · Capacity

One card does up to 11 times the work of one request at a time.

A graphics card can work on many requests in the same pass. I raised the load until users would start to wait: the first word within 2 seconds, then at least 10 words a second, for 99 % of calls.

Inside that limit the card finished 4,340 agent questions an hour with 16 agents at once, against 540 with one at a time. In an 8-hour day that is about 34,700 questions, 12 times the break-even.

For long solutions the margin is thin: the card finishes about 9,050 in 8 hours, against a break-even of 5,350. That only pays if the machine also works at night.

Capacity is rarely the problem. Check it for long answers, and plan work for the quiet hours.

Finding 4 · Quality

A busy card answers as well as an idle one.

The worry is obvious: if the card shares its work across many requests, answers could get worse. Every answer was scored against a fixed key, 131,939 in total, with no AI judging another AI.

Under load the card answered 99 % of the agent questions correctly, against 99 % with one at a time. On all four tasks the difference stayed within 3.3 points, and the two small drops come from tiny samples at one request at a time (18 long solutions).

One limit: my card runs a compressed, 4-bit copy of the model. I did not compare it with the full-precision version a cloud may serve.

Load does not cost quality. Test the compressed model on your own tasks before you rely on it.

Finding 5 · Memory

Memory, not speed, set the limit. My hypothesis did not hold.

On your own card no token is billed, so an agent’s re-sent context is free. I expected more: that the card keeps earlier context in a cache and barely has to work on it again. My hypothesis before the runs: “at least 80 % from cache”.

I measured 29 % with one agent and 0.6 % with 16. The model’s weights take most of the card’s 32 GB. What is left holds about 95,000 tokens of context, it was full from 16 agents on, and the server never ran more than 19 requests at once.

A smaller model shows the trade. gpt-oss-20b left five times the memory, served 64 agents at once without reaching a limit, and took 84 % of its input from cache. It answered 86 % of the agent questions correctly, against 99 %.

If you own, check the memory left after the model before you look at speed.

Finding 6 · What changes the answer

The answer depends on people, not on the electricity price.

The 2,850 counts only the machine and its electricity. Each bar shows that number with one thing changed. A longer bar means you need more work before owning pays off.

People matter most: 5 hours of care a month cost €10.80 a day, more than the machine, and raise the number to 7,500. A spare machine raises it to 5,700. A cloud that bills repeated text at a quarter raises it to 8,900, because an agent sends the same text again at every step.

Electricity barely matters: between €0.15 and €0.35 per kWh, the number stays between 2,700 and 3,000.

Before you buy, count the people and the spare machine. The electricity price can wait.

  • Below the break-even: use the API. It costs nothing when nobody asks.
  • Above it: own, and fill the quiet hours with batch work, such as long documents at night.
  • Watch your agents. They multiply tokens per task and an API bills every one, so they are what moves a team towards the line.
  • If your data may not leave the building, owning is the only option, and the question becomes whether you can keep the card busy. The cloud in this comparison is already German.

  • Hardware. One NVIDIA RTX 5090 with 32 GB in my own PC, bought privately. No sponsor, no employer hardware, no employer data. This is not a recommendation to buy this card: the method works for any card.
  • Load. 1, 4, 16, 32 and 64 requests at once, then two levels in between where the limit lies, two runs per level, 45 seconds warm-up and 240 seconds measured.
  • The waiting limit. A call counts as good when its first word arrives within 2 seconds and the answer then flows at 10 words a second or more [4]. A load level counts only if 99 % of its calls are good.
  • Quality. Every answer is compared with a fixed key. No AI judges another AI. An answer cut off at the length limit counts as wrong.
  • Prices. The API side is the public price list of STACKIT, a German cloud, for the same open models, without VAT [1]. I did not run anything against that API. Electricity is the German business price [2], labour the German average cost per hour worked [3].
  • Power. Read at the wall by a smart plug, for the whole machine: 85 W when idle with the model loaded, 500 to 740 W under load.

Limits

A measurement of this setup, not of GPUs in general.

  • One machine, one night. One consumer card, two runs per load level. Another card, driver or software version will give other capacities.
  • The limit of 19 requests is specific. It comes from how this version of the serving software divides the card’s memory for this model. Another version may fit more.
  • A compressed model. The 27B model ran as a 4-bit copy and was not compared with full precision.
  • List prices, no API run. The API side is a price list applied to the tokens my card counted. Its speed and its answers were not measured.
  • Even load. The test keeps a constant number of requests in flight. Real traffic comes in bursts, which lowers what a card can take.
  • Benchmarks stand in for work. The tasks have answer keys, real business tasks rarely do. The agent task is synthetic.
  • A simple cost model. No cost of capital, no resale value, no spare machine, no room or cooling in the base case. The table under “Full results” shows what each would change.
