Experiment 2 · November 2026

Count your tasks before you buy a GPU.

Should a team buy a graphics card for its AI models, or pay per token? I loaded my own card until users would start to wait and priced the same work at a German cloud. The answer is a number of tasks a day.

  • 2 open models
  • 4 tasks
  • 84 runs
  • 131,939 answers scored against a key
  • 1 RTX 5090
  • Run on 13 November 2026
Code and data on GitHub

Finding 1 · The break-even

Owning pays from about 2,850 agent questions a working day.

An API bills every question. A machine you own costs the same every day, busy or idle. So the two lines cross at a number of tasks a day.

An agent question, one that looks things up with tools, costs about a quarter of a cent at the API. My machine and its power cost €6.60 a working day, so the lines cross at 2,850. Add 5 hours of care a month at €45, and they cross at 7,500.

Count your tasks per working day before you compare prices per token.

Finding 2 · By task

Agents reach the line first. A team’s chat never does.

What decides is the API’s price for one task. An agent sends its whole context again at every step, so 89 % of its price is input, and owning pays from 2,850 a day.

Short answers need 22,400 a day, sorted messages 32,400, long solutions 5,350. A team of 50 asking 20 short questions each sends 1,000 a day: a twentieth of the line.

Compare each kind of task with its own break-even. One number for “AI usage” hides the answer.

Finding 3 · Capacity

One card does up to 11 times the work of one request at a time.

A graphics card can work on many requests in one pass. I raised the load until users would start to wait: the first word within 2 seconds, then 10 words a second, for 99 % of calls.

At that limit the card finished 4,340 agent questions an hour, against 540 one at a time: about 34,700 in an 8-hour day, 12 times the break-even. Long solutions are tight: 9,050 in 8 hours against a break-even of 5,350.

Capacity is rarely the problem. Check it for long answers, and plan work for the quiet hours.

Finding 4 · Quality

A busy card answers as well as an idle one.

If the card shares its work across many requests, answers could get worse. I scored all 131,939 answers against a fixed key, with no AI judging another AI.

Under load the card answered 99 % of the agent questions correctly, against 99 % one at a time. On all four tasks the gap stayed within 3.3 points. The card ran a 4-bit copy of the model, which I did not compare with full precision.

Load does not cost quality. Test the compressed model on your own tasks before you rely on it.

Finding 5 · Memory

Memory, not speed, set the limit. My hypothesis did not hold.

On your own card an agent’s re-sent context is free, and I expected at least 80 % of it to come from the card’s cache. I measured 29 % with one agent and 0.6 % with 16: the model’s weights fill most of the 32 GB, and the server never ran more than 19 requests at once.

The smaller gpt-oss-20b left five times the memory, served 64 agents at once and took 84 % from cache. It answered 86 % of the agent questions correctly, against 99 %.

If you own, check the memory left after the model before you look at speed.

Finding 6 · What changes the answer

The answer depends on people, not on the electricity price.

Each bar shows the 2,850 with one thing changed. People matter most: 5 hours of care a month raise it to 7,500. A spare machine raises it to 5,700, a cloud that bills repeated text at a quarter to 8,900.

Electricity barely matters: between €0.15 and €0.35 per kWh, the number stays between 2,700 and 3,000.

Before you buy, count the people and the spare machine. The electricity price can wait.

How to count for your team.

The formula has one line. Everything in it is a number you can look up.

Break-even tasks per working day = cost of owning per working day ÷ (API price per task − electricity per task)

How the servers were priced

Break-even per working day = (hardware price ÷ 3 years + idle power × 8,760 hours × €0.2366 per kWh + colocation a year) ÷ 250 working days ÷ API price per task. The method is the same in every column; the inputs change, and so does the task: the card is priced per measured agent question, the servers per coding task. Servers: 8 B200 at $447,238 as configured at Exxact [20], 8 B300 at $565,151 starting price on Supermicro’s US store [17], at $1.1127 per € [19], without import duty or VAT. Idle power: 2.9 kW as measured on an 8-GPU B200 server [18]; no B300 figure is published, so the B200 value stands in. Colocation: Frankfurt’s €225 per kW and month for 250 to 500 kW [22], a lower bound for a single server, for 12.5 kW (the B200 server under full load [18]) and 14 kW (DGX B300 [21]). Coding task: one commit of a published single-developer case study, GLM-5.1 and 5.2 on B200s [16]: 367 commits, per commit 333,712 new input, 2.12 million cached input and 13,787 output tokens, priced at Z.ai’s GLM-5.3 list price [15]. Not included: networking, electricity under load, and capacity: whether one server serves that many developers was not measured. python analysis/cost.py --examples in the repository prints the same table.

  • Below the break-even: use the API. It costs nothing when nobody asks.
  • Above it: own, and fill the quiet hours with batch work.
  • Watch your agents. They multiply tokens per task, and an API bills every one.
  • If your data may not leave the building, owning is the only option. The cloud here is already German.

  • Hardware. One NVIDIA RTX 5090 with 32 GB in my own PC, bought privately: no sponsor, no employer hardware or data.
  • Load. 1 to 64 requests at once, two runs per level, 4 minutes measured after 45 seconds of warm-up.
  • The waiting limit. A level counts only if 99 % of calls get their first word within 2 seconds and then 10 words a second or more [4].
  • Quality. Every answer is compared with a fixed key. An answer cut off at the length limit counts as wrong.
  • Prices. The API side is STACKIT’s public list price for the same models, without VAT [1]; electricity is the German business price [2], labour the German average cost per hour [3].
  • Power. Read at the wall for the whole machine: 85 W idle with the model loaded, 500 to 740 W under load.

Limits

A measurement of this setup, not of GPUs in general.

  • One machine, one night. Another card, driver or software version gives other capacities.
  • The limit of 19 requests is specific to how this software version divides the card’s memory.
  • A compressed model. 4-bit, not compared with full precision.
  • List prices, no API run. The API’s speed and answers were not measured.
  • Even load. Real traffic comes in bursts, which lowers what a card can take.
  • Benchmarks stand in for work. Real tasks rarely have answer keys, and the agent task is synthetic.
  • Not a test of coding agents on large models. GLM-5.3 has 753 billion parameters [14] and needs a multi-card server, so the server example above is illustrative.
  • A simple cost model. No cost of capital, resale value, spare machine, room or cooling in the base case; “Full results” shows what each changes.
